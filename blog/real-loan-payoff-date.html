Your Real Loan Payoff Date (And Why the Lender's Estimate Is Wrong)

James Whitmore
March 17, 2026 Payoff
Article Middle Ad — Loading...

You took out a $15,000 loan at 9% APR for 48 months. Your payment is around $373. The lender says you'll be paid off in 48 months. They're probably wrong.

Why? Because the payoff date assumes you never pay late, never pay extra, and never have a change in interest rate (if it's variable). But real life happens.

One late payment can add months to your term. Most lenders apply late fees first, then interest, then principal. If you're late, your principal doesn't go down as fast. You end up paying more interest than planned.

On the flip side, paying extra can shorten your term dramatically. Adding $50 a month to that $15,000 loan would cut around 8 months off the term and save around $400 in interest.

I had a client named Lisa who was convinced her auto loan would be paid off in 36 months. She showed me the paperwork. Then I asked how much she was paying each month. She was paying $20 less than the required payment because she'd "rounded down" on her automatic transfer. She thought it wouldn't matter.

It mattered. At that lower payment, her loan would take around 48 months — a full year longer. She literally didn't believe me until I showed her the amortization schedule.

Also, check if your loan has a "simple interest" or "precomputed interest" structure. Most personal loans use simple interest, which means you save interest by paying early. Precomputed loans charge you all the interest upfront. If you pay early on a precomputed loan, you don't save anything. Those are rare now, but they still exist.

Here's my rule: request an amortization schedule when you sign the loan. Then make a plan. If you can pay extra, even $25 a month, do it. And if you get a raise or a bonus, put part of it toward the loan.

Lisa ended up increasing her payment to the correct amount and adding $25 extra. She paid off her loan in 32 months. She saved around $300 in interest. That's not life-changing, but it's a nice dinner.

Don't trust the lender's estimate. Calculate your own payoff date. Run the numbers. You might be surprised.
