APR vs. Interest Rate: The Difference Banks Don't Want You to Know

James Whitmore
May 04, 2026 Rates
Article Middle Ad — Loading...

Here's a number that makes me angrier than it should: around 72% of people can't explain the difference between APR and interest rate. I know because I used to quiz clients. Most just stared at me.

Let me fix that right now.

The interest rate is what the bank charges you for borrowing money. The APR (annual percentage rate) is that PLUS fees — origination charges, points, underwriting fees, sometimes even required insurance. Banks advertise the interest rate because it's lower. The APR is the real cost.

Look, I literally saw a loan offer last month with 6.9% interest and 8.2% APR. That gap is around $1,800 in fees on a $30,000 loan. The borrower had no idea. He thought he was getting a "great rate."

He wasn't.

I had a client named Dave last year. He was comparing two personal loan offers for a home renovation. One had a 7.2% interest rate with $1,200 in fees. The other had 8.1% interest with $250 in fees. He was ready to take the lower rate because it looked cheaper. I showed him the APR math. The second offer was around $500 less overall. He was shocked. Bankers hate when you know this stuff.

The trick is: ignore the interest rate at first. Ask for the APR. Then compare APRs across different offers. That's the only apples-to-apples comparison.

Now here's the part that really gets me: some banks still advertise "no fee" loans. That's not possible. There's always a fee somewhere. They just bury it in a higher interest rate or a prepayment penalty. A true "no fee" loan would lose money. So if you see that phrase, start asking questions.

One more thing: credit unions often have lower APRs than big banks for the same credit profile. I'm not saying switch everything to a credit union. But when you're shopping for a personal loan or auto loan, include one in your search. Around 30% of my clients find a better deal there.

The Fed is supposed to meet again around September. If they cut rates, you might see lower APRs on new loans. But don't wait for them. Run the numbers now. If you find a better offer later, you can always refinance — as long as there's no prepayment penalty.

I will keep posting updates on this. Check back soon.

P.S. I forgot to mention: Dave ended up taking the second offer and saved enough to buy new kitchen cabinets. He sent me a photo. I smiled for an hour.
