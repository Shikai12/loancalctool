Three Stories About Debt Nobody Talks About

James Whitmore
April 16, 2026 Debt
Article Middle Ad — Loading...

I can't sleep sometimes. Not because of my own money problems — I'm fine. But because of the conversations I have during the day.

People tell me things they haven't told their spouses. Debts they've hidden. Payment notices they've thrown away. Calls they've ignored.

Here are three stories. Names changed. Details changed enough to protect them. But the emotions are real.

Story 1: The Car Repossession

A man in his 40s — let's call him Marcus — came to me with a repo on his credit report from three years ago. He'd lost his job, fallen behind on his truck payment, and the bank took it. He was embarrassed. He didn't tell his wife until the truck was gone. She thought it was stolen.

We negotiated a settlement with the bank for 50% of the remaining balance. He paid it off with a second job at a grocery store. His credit is still recovering, but his marriage is better. He said, "The truck was just metal. Her trust was everything."

Story 2: The Hidden Card

A woman — call her Theresa — had a credit card her husband didn't know about. $9,000 balance at 23% APR. She used it for kids' clothes, groceries, and some online shopping she was too ashamed to admit. She was making minimum payments. The interest was eating her alive.

She came to me alone. We consolidated the debt into a personal loan at 11% with a credit union. Then she told her husband. He was angry for a weekend. Then he helped her pay it off. They're now debt-free except their mortgage. She told me, "I wish I'd told him years ago."

Story 3: The Student Loan Spiral

Derek (not the same Derek as before) had $120,000 in student loans for a degree he never finished. He'd been in default for four years. His wages were being garnished. He was suicidal.

We got him on an income-driven repayment plan. Payment: $0 because his income was so low (he worked part-time at a bookstore). The default was cured. His credit is still bad, but he's not getting garnished anymore. He's sleeping at night. He's seeing a therapist. He's not out of the woods, but he's walking.

Here's what I've learned: debt is not a moral failing. It's a math problem with emotional consequences. The shame is often worse than the interest rate.

If you're struggling, talk to someone. A partner. A friend. A financial counselor (not a debt settlement company). A therapist. The silence is what kills you. The debt can be fixed.

I'm not a therapist. I'm not a social worker. I'm a guy who's seen a lot of spreadsheets and heard a lot of confessions. The spreadsheets are easier. The confessions are heavier.

But I keep doing this because I've also seen people come out the other side. Marcus is driving a paid-off used car. Theresa just celebrated her 10-year anniversary. Derek is writing again — he's working on a novel.

You can get out too.

I will keep posting updates on this. Check back soon.

P.S. I'm going to take Cooper for a walk now. He doesn't care about debt. He cares about sticks. There's a lesson there, maybe.
